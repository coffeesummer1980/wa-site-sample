Kokoro · the heart

Why Cleaning a Toilet Polishes the Heart

A Japanese kimono merchant on the quiet power of everyday cleaning — and what it teaches about kokoro

By Noriyuki Ikeda  |  Based on his talk “The Toilet and the Japanese” (Oct 22, 2026)  |  Nov 17, 2026
The short answerYou cannot see your heart, so you cannot polish it directly. Instead, you polish the things right in front of you. In Japan, the best teacher for this has long been the toilet — the place everyone uses many times a day and no one wants to clean. Cleaning it with your own hands, by your own choice, day after day, makes you humble, makes you notice, and grows a sense of wonder and gratitude.

I run a kimono shop in western Japan. People often ask me what kimono and toilet cleaning could possibly have in common. My answer is simple: both are ways the Japanese have traditionally polished the heart. In Japanese we call it kokoro — the heart, the mind, the spirit, all in one word.

"Cleaning the toilet polishes your heart" may sound like a moral slogan. But I believe it is a very reasonable idea that ordinary Japanese people used to take for granted. Let me explain it through two stories.

01A company president who cleaned the toilets alone

The first story is about Hidesaburo Kagiyama, the founder of Yellow Hat, one of Japan's largest auto parts retail chains.

When he was young, Kagiyama worked for a company that sold car parts. It was the era of Japan's rapid economic growth. Many customers were rough young men who loved modifying their cars, and the sales style was just as rough. Kagiyama disliked it, so he started his own business. He refused to sell parts for illegal modifications and wanted to build a shop where ordinary drivers — including women — could buy parts with peace of mind.

But the economy was booming. Jobs were everywhere, and it was hard to hire anyone. The people who did come were often restless and troubled. Hoping to calm the atmosphere even a little, Kagiyama began cleaning the company toilets by himself, every single day.

His employees would use the toilet right next to him as he scrubbed. Some even complained: "It's awkward for us when the boss does that." He kept going anyway, alone.

After ten years, all of his employees were cleaning together. After twenty years, other companies were asking to visit as part of their staff training. People began to say there was a way in cleaning — a dō, like budō (the way of martial arts) or sadō (the way of tea). It came to be called sōji-dō, "the way of cleaning." The movement later became a nonprofit organization, and it has spread beyond Japan.

Kagiyama is known for cleaning toilets mostly with his bare hands, because that way he can feel the dirt. He does use tools for stains that won't come off by hand. But the basic attitude is to touch it, and make it clean, yourself.

02A school that changed after four cleanings

The movement reached schools, too. In those days there were schools so troubled that nearly every window was broken. Volunteers would go to such a school and announce, "Please gather on this day, at this time. Let's all clean together." Students, teachers, and adults scrubbed the whole school side by side.

They did this once every few months — one, two, three, four times. By about the fourth cleaning, the school changed. Changing society through cleaning: that was what Kagiyama and his companions set out to do.

03The five benefits of cleaning

Kagiyama summarized what cleaning does for a person in five points.

1. You become humble. It is hard to be arrogant while you are voluntarily cleaning a toilet. Taking on the job people dislike most naturally makes you humble.

2. You become someone who notices. A toilet can look clean from above. But when you crouch down, you see dirt everywhere. Some things only become visible from a low position. Keep cleaning, and you start to notice what others overlook — at home and at work.

3. You develop a true sense of wonder. Excitement at a concert, or calm after an inspiring talk, lasts only a moment. Kagiyama says that is not true wonder. True wonder comes from watching something used many times a day become clean through your own hands — doing it yourself, and feeling it yourself.

4. Gratitude begins to grow. Gratitude grows in people who can feel wonder. Without that sense of wonder, gratitude struggles to take root.

5. Your heart is polished. And finally, the heart is polished. Since it cannot be polished directly, you polish what is close to you. Little by little, the heart changes.

04You polish what you can see, because the heart is invisible

This idea — polish the things around you to polish your heart — did not start with Kagiyama. It is something Japanese people have long taken for granted.

Traditionally, the Japanese felt that gods dwell in all things, and they gave thanks to them. There is even a god of the toilet: Ususama Myōō (known in Sanskrit as Ucchuṣma). Because a god lives there, you keep it clean. And by keeping it clean, you polish your own heart. I believe this feeling lies at the root of the Japanese love of cleanliness that visitors to Japan often notice.

Tea ceremony and martial arts work the same way. In Japan we say that tea and Zen are one, and that martial arts and Zen are one. By devoting yourself fully to a single practice, you polish the heart. For me, wearing kimono — taking care of it and wearing it with respect — is another such practice.

05What Takeshi Kitano's mentor told him

The second story is one I heard about Takeshi Kitano — known in Japan as the comedian Beat Takeshi, and around the world as an award-winning film director.

Kitano has reportedly said that he does not think he is especially talented. But when he looks back on why he has been so highly praised, there is one thing he can think of.

When he started out as a young comedian in Asakusa, Tokyo, the first thing his mentor told him was this:

"Listen. From now on, whenever you use a toilet, leave it cleaner than you found it."

Kitano kept doing that his whole life. He said it is the only reason he can think of for how far he has come.

Cleaning, I believe, truly is a way.

06Where to start

You don't need to clean with bare hands, and you don't need a special place. Start with the words of Kitano's mentor: leave the toilet cleaner than you found it. At home, at work, at a café — you can begin today.

07Words to take with you

The heart cannot be polished directly. So polish what is close to you.

Cleaning a toilet or wearing a kimono — either one can polish the heart. This old Japanese sensibility is slowly fading, even in Japan. If it speaks to you, I would be happy if you kept it alive and passed it on to the next generation, wherever in the world you are.

ABOUT THE AUTHOR

Noriyuki Ikeda

A kimono merchant. Born in Kyoto in 1962 and a graduate of Doshisha University's Faculty of Law, he spent ten years preparing for Japan's bar exam before entering the kimono trade at 33. He now runs Wasoukan, a group of kimono stores in Tottori and Shimane, and shares the theme “Bringing the Japanese heart to the world, and to the future” through talks, radio and writing.
